How much precision survives missing data?

library(precisionenvelope)

The question

A study expects a quarter of its outcomes to be missing. Is the planned precision still within reach? The overall missingness rate cannot answer this on its own. Under missing at random (MAR), the efficient variance depends on which units go missing: losing outcomes where the residual variance is small costs little, and losing them where it is large costs a great deal. Rules of thumb that tolerate a fixed percentage of missing data treat every mechanism with that rate alike.

The package implements the precision envelope of Nakamura (2026), which replaces the rule of thumb with a calculation. For a stated overall observation rate \(p\) and a lower bound \(\pi_L\) on the observation probability, it returns the sharp range of efficient variance that the admissible MAR mechanisms can produce. Against a precision requirement, each rate is then guaranteed (every admissible mechanism meets the requirement), allocation-sensitive (some mechanisms meet it and others do not) or inadmissible (no admissible mechanism meets it).

Planning without data

At the design stage the inputs are the share \(R^2\) of the outcome variance explained by the always observed covariates, the lower bound \(\pi_L\), and a law for the residual variance \(b(W) = \mathrm{Var}(Y \mid W)\). With a constant residual variance, MCAR is the favorable mechanism, and the envelope reduces to the interval between MCAR and the adverse mechanism.

env <- precision_envelope(p = c(0.95, 0.85, 0.75, 0.65), lower = 0.10,
                          r2 = 0.30, target = 0.80)

When the residual variance differs across covariate profiles, the favorable mechanism can do better than MCAR, because it concentrates the missing outcomes where they carry little information. A gamma law with a stated coefficient of variation is a convenient planning assumption; fitted values from a pilot study can be used instead.

het <- precision_envelope(p = c(0.95, 0.85, 0.75, 0.65), lower = 0.10,
                          r2 = 0.30, b = residual_law_gamma(cv = 0.8),
                          target = 0.80)

The analytic illustration

Section 5.1 of Nakamura (2026) uses a residual variance that takes the values 0.1 and 1.9 on two regions of equal mass, with \(A = B = 1\), \(\pi_L = 0.2\) and a requirement of 80% retention. At 75% observation the favorable allocation raises the standard error by 1.2% and MCAR by 8.0%.

ill <- precision_envelope(p = 0.75, lower = 0.2, A = 1, b = c(0.1, 1.9),
                          target = 0.80)
ill$table[, c("q", "se_inflation_favorable", "se_inflation_mcar",
              "se_inflation_adverse", "phase")]
#>      q se_inflation_favorable se_inflation_mcar se_inflation_adverse
#> 1 0.25             0.01242284        0.08012345            0.4790199
#>                  phase
#> 1 allocation-sensitive
ill$thresholds
#> guaranteed       mcar   possible 
#> 0.05263158 0.33333333 0.52000000

The thresholds are \(q_G^* = 5.3\%\), \(q_{MCAR}^* = 33.3\%\) and \(q_P^* = 52.0\%\). When the mechanism may separate units within a region, the adverse endpoint at 25% missing raises the standard error by 47.9%. When the observation probability can depend only on the region label, each region is an indivisible stratum. The adverse endpoint is then the allocation that observes the high-variance region half the time, which raises the standard error by 21.4%, and \(q_G^*\) rises to 17.2%.

strata <- precision_envelope(p = 0.75, lower = 0.2, A = 1, b = c(0.1, 1.9),
                             covariate = "strata", target = 0.80)
strata$table[, c("q", "rho_adverse", "se_inflation_adverse")]
#>      q rho_adverse se_inflation_adverse
#> 1 0.25   0.6779661            0.2144958
strata$thresholds
#> guaranteed       mcar   possible

Planning targeted recovery

Starting from that allocation, in which the high-variance half is observed only half the time, the cheapest way to restore 80% retention is to recover part of the missing outcomes in that half, following the cost-adjusted square-root rule. With equal costs, recovering 7.76 outcomes per 100 participants raises the observation rate to 82.76%.

plan <- recovery_plan(b = c(0.1, 1.9), baseline = c(1, 0.5), A = 1,
                      target = 0.80)
plan

Estimating the envelope from data

envelope_fit() estimates the envelope for the mean of a partially observed continuous outcome. The example uses the MASLD cases of the public survival::nafld1 data, with baseline body mass index as the outcome and age and sex as covariates.

nafld1 <- survival::nafld1
cases <- nafld1[!is.na(nafld1$case.id) & nafld1$id == nafld1$case.id, ]
dat <- data.frame(bmi = as.numeric(cases$bmi), age = as.numeric(cases$age),
                  male = as.integer(cases$male == 1))
dat$age_centered_sq <- (dat$age - mean(dat$age))^2

set.seed(20260901)
fit <- envelope_fit(dat, outcome = "bmi",
                    covariates = c("age", "male", "age_centered_sq"),
                    p = c(0.70, 0.75, 0.80, 0.85, 0.90, 0.97),
                    lower = 0.05, target = 0.80)
fit

The realized mechanism retained about 86% of the information, close to the favorable endpoint at the same observation rate. At the same 15% missingness, the adverse endpoint in the table shows how much less an admissible mechanism could have retained.

The point classification ignores sampling error. envelope_bootstrap() refits every nuisance model on resampled units and certifies a phase only when a simultaneous band excludes the others. In practice use R = 999; 199 refits keep this vignette fast.

set.seed(20260901)
band <- envelope_bootstrap(fit, R = 199)
band

What the package does not do

The package does not impute missing values, does not improve the estimate of the target, and does not test MAR. The lower bound \(\pi_L\) is an assumption stated by the analyst; the fitted share of observation probabilities below it is reported only as a diagnostic.

Reference
